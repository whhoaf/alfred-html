Capital Market Expectations Part 1: Framework and Macro

Capital Market Expectations

p.8
  • Apply economic tools to form capital market expectations. This is applied economics, not the theory of Levels I and II.
  • Client objectives and constraints + capital market expectations (expected return, sigma, correlations of asset classes) feed the SAA.
  • No forecasting tool for risky assets works consistently at all times; multiple tools will disagree, which itself is valuable insight into consensus or its absence.

Challenges in Forecasting

p.9
  1. Economic data limitations: lags, revisions, methodology changes, index rebasing.
  2. Data measurement: transcription errors, survivorship bias, appraisal (smoothed) data.
  3. Historical estimate limits: how long a data set to use, given regime changes.
  4. Ex post (realized) risk understates ex ante (anticipated) risk.
  5. Biased methods: data mining, time-period bias.
  6. Ignoring conditioning information, e.g. beta varying through the business cycle.
  7. Misinterpreting correlations.
  8. Psychological biases: anchoring (to initial data or estimates), status quo (extrapolating recent past), confirmation, overconfidence, prudence (avoiding extreme forecasts), availability (easily recalled events).
  9. Model, parameter, and input uncertainty.

Economic Growth

p.10
  • The trend line's slope is the trend growth rate; over the long term, average bond returns and equity capital appreciation must anchor to trend growth.
  • Basic model: trend growth = growth in labor input (population growth + participation growth) + growth in capital per worker + growth in total factor productivity.
  • The output gap between actual and trend GDP is associated with a declining inflation rate.
  • Exogenous shocks disrupt trend growth: policy changes, political events, technological progress, natural disasters, new natural resources, financial crises.

Forecasting Approaches

p.11
  • Econometrics (multiple regression): complex and slow; structural and reduced-form variants; danger of estimation error and regime change breaking relationships.
  • Economic indicators: simple and available; leading indicators for turning points; a diffusion index shows the share of indicators pointing up or down; can give false signals, and revisions may overfit the past.
  • Checklist: simple but subjective and time-consuming.

Business Cycle Phases (1): Recovery to Late Expansion

p.12
  • Initial recovery (usually short): stimulative policy, rising business confidence, decelerating inflation; short rates low, long rates bottoming; stocks may rise briskly anticipating recovery; cyclical and riskier assets do well.
  • Early expansion: unemployment falls, output gap shrinks, consumers borrow and spend, investment and profits rise fast; policy less stimulative; short rates rising, long rates stable or slightly up, curve flattening; stocks do well.
  • Late expansion: output gap closed, capacity pressure boosts investment, boom mentality, inflation rising; policy turning restrictive; short and long rates rise (long more slowly, curve keeps flattening); stocks rise but volatile; cyclicals may underperform.

Business Cycle Phases (2): Slowdown and Contraction

p.13
  • Slowdown: rising rates, fewer viable investments, accumulated debt; confidence wavers, inflation still rising, policy turns neutral; rates peak then decline, curve may invert (especially if the central bank keeps pushing short rates up); credit spreads widen (weaker credits most); stocks decline.
  • Contraction (typically 12–18 months): weak confidence, investment drops, major corporate failures, profits fall sharply; policy eases; spreads widen until signs of the turn; rates decline and the curve steepens substantially; stocks fall but rise in later stages anticipating the turn.
  • Caveats: phase lengths and amplitudes vary; cyclical vs secular effects are hard to separate; market response is as uncertain as the cycle; inflation expectations tend to be countercyclical.

Inflation and Asset Classes

p.14
  • Outright deflation is widely seen as damaging (nominal debt harder to service, stimulative monetary policy less effective); moderate inflation imposes only modest costs.
  • Inflation within expectations: cash earns the real rate; bond short yields move more than long; equity unaffected given predictable growth; real estate neutral.
  • Inflation above expectations: cash does well as yields rise; nominal bonds do poorly; equity does poorly except pass-through companies; real estate does well as values rise.
  • Deflation: cash returns near 0% but purchasing power rises; bonds attractive if no default; equity poor with falling activity and asset values; real estate poor with falling property values.

Government Policy

p.15
  • Monetary: stimulative = money supply up, short rates down; contractionary the reverse; short rates indirectly move long rates. Crucial in the short to medium term.
  • Fiscal: stimulative = bigger deficit (G minus T); automatic stabilizers damp the cycle. Key in the long run.
Taylor rule (next change in short rates): real Rtarget = policy-neutral real rate + 0.5 × (real GDP growthexpected − real GDP growthtrend) + 0.5 × (inflationexpected − inflationtarget) nominal Rtarget = real Rtarget + inflationexpected

Negative Rates; Policy and the Yield Curve

p.16
  • Negative rates associate with deflation and are seen as unsustainable, yet persisted after the 2007–08 recession despite QE. They complicate forecasting: what risk-free rate? Options: use the Taylor-rule policy-neutral rate, forecast paths to a positive long-term risk-free rate, or assume the economy sits in early recovery.
  • Policy mix and the curve: both fiscal and monetary expansive means the economy should improve and the curve slopes sharply upward in anticipation; both restrictive means contraction ahead and a downward-sloping curve.

International Linkages and EM Warning Signs

p.17
  • Macro linkages hit smaller, less diversified economies harder. Current and capital accounts link: (X − M) = (S − I) + (T − G). A tax cut (T down) raises the current account deficit and the capital account surplus; partner economies grow as export demand rises. Interest rates and currencies also link.
  • Emerging market warning signs:
    1. Irresponsible policy: government deficit above 4% of GDP.
    2. Insufficient growth: real growth below 4%.
    3. Overvalued currency, twin deficits: current account deficit above 4% of GDP.
    4. Excessive foreign-currency debt: foreign debt above 50% of GDP, or above 200% of current account receipts.
    5. Thin short-term liquidity: FX reserves below 100% of short-term foreign-currency debt.
    6. Risky politics; policies unsupportive of long-term growth.

Capital Market Expectations Part 2: Forecasting Asset Class Returns

Forecasting Toolkit

p.19
  • Statistical: historical sample data, possibly with a shrinkage estimate (weighted average of sample and an alternative source such as prior knowledge); time-series analysis (e.g. volatility clustering).
  • Discounted cash flow. Equity via Grinold-Kroner: expected return = dividend yield + buybacks + nominal earnings growth + repricing.
Grinold-Kroner: E(R) = (D/P − %ΔS) + %ΔE + %Δ(P/E) note: %ΔE − %ΔS = growth rate of EPS
  • Bonds: YTM, refined with modified duration and projected rate changes. Rule of thumb: if holding period = Macaulay duration, total return is little affected by yield changes (immunized); shorter horizon = price risk dominates (rising rates hurt); longer = reinvestment risk dominates (falling rates hurt).

Building Blocks and Equilibrium Models

p.20
  • Building blocks for FI: risk-free rate + term premium + credit premium + liquidity premium.
  • Singer-Terhaar equilibrium model: weight the fully integrated and fully segmented risk premiums by the market's degree of integration, then add premiums for nonsystematic risks such as illiquidity.
integrated: RPi = ρi,GM × σi × (RPGM/σGM) [= βi × RPGM] segmented: RPi = σi × Sharpesegmented (RPGM/σGM) = global market Sharpe ratio; assume segmented Sharpe equals global unless told otherwise
  • Also: surveys of experts, and judgment synthesizing many sources.

Forecasting Real Estate Returns

p.21
  • Cycles: boom and bust move property values and lease rates; high-quality properties fluctuate less.
  • Cap rate = NOI / property value, the standard commercial RE metric and its long-term discount rate. Rises with interest rates and vacancy; falls with credit and debt-financing supply.
infinite horizon: E(Rre) = cap rate + NOI growth finite horizon: E(Rre) = cap rate + NOI growth − %Δcap rate
  • Risk premium = term premium (long-lived assets) + credit premium (tenant nonpayment) + equity risk premium (value, lease, vacancy swings) + liquidity premium; the total sits between corporate bonds and equities.
  • In equilibrium models, use unsmoothed data; the illiquidity premium matters a lot; RE may depend more on local than global factors.

Real Estate: Public vs Private, Residential

p.22
  • Diversified direct portfolios are feasible for institutions and the ultra-wealthy; smaller investors hold undiversified direct property or REITs.
  • Correlations: REITs track equities in the short term; direct RE does not; REITs and direct RE converge over the long term.
  • Performance patterns: leverage-adjusted, REITs beat direct RE with lower volatility. Among REITs, retail highest return with second-lowest volatility (apartments lowest volatility), industrial lowest return and highest volatility. Direct: apartments highest return, offices lowest.
  • Residential is the largest developed-property class (about 75% of global value). Beat equities inflation-adjusted with lower volatility over 70 years, though weaker in the past 40. Returns uncorrelated across countries, while equity correlations keep rising.

Forecasting Exchange Rates

p.23
  • Trade and current account focus. Trade flows rarely move FX much, but large trade-related capital flows can trigger crises. PPP (expected FX change = expected inflation differential) fails short term, works better long term; undermined by trade barriers, nontraded goods, capital flows. Capital-flow restrictions make FX more sensitive to persistent unsustainable current account imbalances; even without restrictions, a large persistent deficit weakens the currency over time to induce SAA shifts into it.
  • Capital flow focus. Adjustments hit rates hard.
capital mobility: E(%ΔSd/f) = (rd−rf) + (termd−termf) + (creditd−creditf) + (equityd−equityf) + (liquidd−liquidf) uncovered IRP: E(%ΔSd/f) = id − if (carry trades defy UIP)
  • Short term, capital flows into currencies of countries in the strongest cycle phase; long term, rapid growth draws in global investors who raise SAA weight as the currency weakens.

Forecasting Volatility (VCV Matrices)

p.24
  • Sample VCV matrix: reliable only when observations are at least 10× the number of assets; estimates are unbiased but accurate only in very large samples, with no guarantee of mutual consistency.
  • Factor-based VCV: variances and covariances derived from sensitivities to a few common factors plus the factor VCV. Needs far fewer observations and forces consistency, but the factor model is almost surely misspecified: precise but biased and inconsistent estimates.
  • Shrinkage estimation: each element a weighted average of the sample matrix and a target (likely factor-based) matrix, same weights throughout; improves efficiency vs sample alone.

Smoothed Returns and Volatility Clustering

p.25
  • Appraisal-based valuations (private RE, PE, hedge funds) understate observed volatility and distort correlations. Unsmoothing techniques exist: if the observed return is lambda times the previous observed return plus (1 − lambda) times the true return, true variance = observed variance × (1 + λ)/(1 − λ).
  • ARCH models forecast volatility because high and low volatility cluster:
σt² = γ + (α + β)σt-1² + β(ηt² − σt-1²) η = this period's return surprise; β controls how much the shock feeds into current volatility

Overview of Asset Allocation

Strategic Asset Allocation

p.28
  • Client objectives and constraints plus capital market expectations produce the SAA. TAA is active management: deviating from the SAA to add value.
  • The SAA matters because its weights determine systematic risk exposure, dominate return differences between portfolios, and anchor TAA.
  • Asset class criteria: internally homogeneous; mutually exclusive; not highly correlated with each other; covering most investable assets; sufficiently liquid. More granular classes blur distinctions but can help TAA.

Investment Governance

p.29
  1. Set long- and short-term investment objectives.
  2. Allocate rights and responsibilities; delegate decisions to the best qualified; complexity grows with program size.
  3. Specify the IPS creation process.
  4. Specify the SAA creation process.
  5. Apply a reporting framework so stakeholders can track performance, compliance, and progress.
  6. Periodic independent governance audit of structure, policies, procedures.
  • Aims: avoid decision-reversal risk; assess staff turnover impact and prevent key person risk.

SAA Approaches; Passive to Active

p.31
  • Asset only: asset risk and return; liabilities enter only via the return objective.
  • Liability relative: directly targets the risk of not funding liabilities, often modeling the surplus (PVA − PVL); typical for institutions with legal obligations.
  • Goals based: subportfolios each built to meet a goal with a set probability and horizon; typical for individuals with quasi-liabilities.
  • Spectrum: most passive implements the SAA with index funds; most active varies from the SAA via TAA plus active management within classes. Tracking error and active return rise along the way. Choose based on constraints (including tax), belief in market efficiency, costs vs benefits, and feasibility given fund size.

Principles of Asset Allocation

Mean-Variance Optimization

p.33–34
  • Software traces the efficient frontier; MVO can optimize assets only, surplus, or risk factors. Any point on the frontier can be found by linear interpolation between the two bracketing corner portfolios (points where an asset class enters or leaves the allocation).
  • Constraints: budget (weights sum to 100%), nonnegativity (no shorts), plus minimum return, maximum risk, weight bounds, liquidity provisions. Good constraints model the real context; constraints added just to tame the output signal that inputs need revisiting.
  • Limitations: output only as good as inputs and highly sensitive to expected-return estimates; tends to produce concentrated allocations in few classes.

Cash, the Risk-Free Asset, and the Optimal Portfolio

p.35–36
  • Over short single periods a risk-free asset exists, giving the capital allocation line tangent to the frontier at portfolio T (in theory the global market portfolio, a natural starting point). In ongoing portfolios, cash is just another low-risk (not zero-risk) class; MVO can be constrained to hold enough cash for client needs.
  • Choosing the point on the frontier:
utility: U = E(R) − 0.005λσ² (% terms) or U = E(R) − 0.5λσ² (decimals) Roy's safety-first: RSF = (E(R) − RMAR) / σ (maximize to minimize probability of breaching the minimum acceptable return)
  • Alternatives: maximize the Sharpe ratio, or select on acceptable return or acceptable risk.

Reverse Optimization and Black-Litterman

p.37
  • Reverse optimization: input world-market weights, back-solve for the implied (consensus) risk premiums, then use those returns to build the frontier. Black-Litterman adjusts the consensus returns for the manager's views and reruns MVO.
  • Pros: world-market weights are diversified and theoretically optimal; less dependence on initial return estimates; resulting portfolios hold more classes.
  • Alternative starting weights: the existing policy portfolio, a peer-group average allocation, or a fundamental weighting scheme.

Monte Carlo and Multiperiod Modeling

p.38
  • MVO ignores multiperiod effects: taxes, rebalancing, path dependency. Monte Carlo simulation fills the gap and focuses attention on long-term outcomes rather than short-term volatility.
  • Pick a single allocation and simulate many random paths (random within set parameters, e.g. normal with 7% mean and 3% sigma, or richer assumptions). Rank paths by probability: e.g. a fan chart for a 50-year-old with $1m shows the 90th/50th/10th percentile wealth paths and where funds run out in the worst decile. Often used in ALM to model the surplus.

Resampling and Nonnormal Optimization

p.39–40
  • Resampled MVO: simulate many input sets from baseline assumptions, build a frontier for each, average the weights, then apply the averaged weights to the baseline. Pros: acknowledges input error; less concentrated portfolios with tighter weight dispersion. Cons: risky allocations can be overdiversified; inherits baseline estimation error; the frontier can have stretches where expected return falls as risk rises; little theory behind it.
  • Nonnormal optimization: models incorporating skew, kurtosis, and asymmetric risk preferences (mean-semivariance, mean-CVaR, mean-variance-skewness, plus kurtosis variants). Justified because returns are often nonnormal, variance is then a flawed risk measure, and investors feel losses about twice as much as equivalent gains.

Incorporating Liabilities

p.41–42
  • Liability characteristics: fixed vs contingent, legal vs quasi-legal, regulation effects; they have duration and convexity; size relative to the investor; drivers of future cash flows (e.g. inflation); timing.
  • Surplus optimization: extend MVO inputs with liability return (discount rate), liability volatility, asset-liability correlations, and constraints. Single period; low to high risk.
  • Two-portfolio approach: a hedging portfolio mimics the liabilities, a return-seeking portfolio adds return; an aggressive version underfunds the hedge while monitoring surplus. Single period; lower risk; any return distribution.
  • Integrated ALM: jointly optimize assets and liabilities. Multiperiod; low to high risk; any distribution.
  • Goals-based: quantify goals (future quasi-liabilities), group by required success probability and payout horizon, pick for each the standard module off the frontier with the highest return whose probability of being exceeded equals the required success probability, discount the goal's cash flows at that module return to size the allocation, then sum modules.

Risk-Factor Allocation and Heuristics

p.43
  • Allocation by risk factors (may diversify better): inputs are factor premiums, sigmas and correlations; factors (CAPM beta, volatility, illiquidity, inflation, duration, FX, default) cut across asset classes; long/short factor portfolios give exposure (long large-cap and short small-cap is net large-cap exposure); not every factor is directly investable.
  • Heuristics: 60/40; equity share = 120 minus age; the endowment (Yale) model overweighting skill-based assets; risk parity, where each class contributes equally to total variance (wi × Cov(ri, rp) = σp²/n; main criticism: ignores expected returns); 1/N equal weighting.

Risk Budgeting

p.44
  • A process specifying which risks to take and how much of each, aiming to maximize return per unit of risk.
MCTRi = βi,portfolio × σp (marginal contribution to total risk) ACTRi = wi × MCTRi (Σ ACTR = portfolio σ) optimal when (Ri − rf) / MCTRi is equal across classes = the portfolio Sharpe ratio

Rebalancing

p.45
  • Restoring the SAA improves long-term return to risk (empirically), but weigh immediate costs against hoped-for benefit.
  • Calendar: easy; unresponsive to sudden moves; may trade unnecessarily when near target or at bad prices.
  • Percentage-range (corridor): reacts to markets but may overreact to noise; cost vs calendar is ambiguous.
    • Wider ranges: higher transaction costs, more risk-tolerant clients, momentum markets.
    • Narrower ranges: more volatile classes (trade off vs costs), lower-correlation classes, mean-reverting markets.

Liquidity Issues in Allocation

p.46
  • Less liquid assets may earn a liquidity premium, lack passive alternatives, and create concentrated exposures; MVO can be constrained to keep them.
  • Modeling options: model the specific assets owned; model the asset class but not the holdings; or exclude them from MVO entirely.

Asset Allocation with Real-World Constraints

Monitoring the SAA; Behavioral Biases

p.48
  • Monitor as the IPS specifies. Changed objectives or constraints: revise the IPS, maybe the SAA. Long-term market changes: adjust the SAA. Short-term changes: consider TAA.
  • Biases and remedies: loss aversion and mental accounting (use goals-based allocation); illusion of control (start from the market portfolio, change only after formal review); representativeness / recency (strong governance, objective allocations); framing (give clients full information); availability, familiarity and home bias (start from the global market portfolio).

Tax Considerations

p.49
rAT = rPT(1 − t) σAT = σPT(1 − t) ρAT = ρPT rebalancing range: DevAT = DevPT / (1 − t)
  • Allocate on after-tax risk and return: effective tax rates differ by class, so tax effects are inconsistent across classes; correlations are untouched.
  • Rebalancing ranges can be wider for taxable investors: a move of DevAT pre-tax has the post-tax effect of DevPT.

Tactical Asset Allocation

p.50
  • Exploits short-term economic or market conditions; deviation size must stay consistent with portfolio risk and constraints; raises trading and tax costs and can concentrate risk.
  • Approaches: discretionary (manager views) or systematic trading rules, chiefly value (value will outperform) and momentum (the trend will continue).

Overview of Equity Portfolio Management

Role of Equities in the Portfolio

p.54
  • Client considerations from the IPS. High risk tolerance suits growth companies; low risk tolerance suits established dividend payers. Managers can also build in client constraints such as ESG.
  • Capital appreciation. Main driver of long-term equity returns; comes from growth in cash flows, revenues, earnings. Equities beat bonds and do best in strong economic growth.
  • Diversification. Less-than-perfect correlation with other asset classes, but the benefit is not constant: in a crisis, correlations and standard deviations both tend to rise.
  • Dividend income. Paid from excess cash flows; recent yields about 1–3%; more stable than total equity returns.
  • Potential inflation hedge. Real equity returns correlate positively with inflation, but the relationship varies over time and by country.

Equity Segmentation

p.55
  • Geography, by stage of macro development: developed (US, UK, Germany), emerging (China, Russia, Brazil), frontier (Iceland, Estonia, Jordan). Helps diversify internationally.

  • Economic activity: market-oriented grouping (markets served, product use, cash flow generation) vs production-oriented grouping (products and inputs).
  • Indexes can combine segmentations (MSCI Europe Large Cap Value) and subdivide by sector (MSCI World Energy).

Equity Portfolio Income

p.56
  • Dividends, typically the largest income source. May face income or withholding tax; optional stock dividends let investors choose cash or shares.
  • Securities lending, often the other side of a short sale. Lender earns a small fee (0.2–0.5% a year), may hold collateral or cash and earn a return on it, and gets the security back later.
  • Option writing for premium income: covered calls, cash-covered puts.
  • Dividend capture: buy just before the ex-dividend date, hold through it, then sell.

Equity Portfolio Costs

p.57
  • Management fees cover research, systems, compliance, trade processing. Usually ad valorem (% of AUM); higher for active portfolios given more analysis and turnover.
  • Performance (incentive) fees, often 10–20% of appreciation above a threshold; a high-water mark stops investors paying twice for the same performance.
  • Other costs: administration fees (corporate actions, voting), marketing and distribution, trading costs, and strategy-specific costs (active funds cost more).

Shareholder Engagement

p.58
  • Investors interact with companies to lift the stock price and improve governance: calls with management, voting at general meetings, raising ESG issues.
  • Active managers engage to improve company performance; index managers focus on minimizing cost. Successful engagement also benefits free riders who never engage.
  • Other stakeholders (employees, customers, creditors, regulators, governments) feel the outcomes too.
  • Activist investors go further: shareholder resolutions, media campaigns, board seats, proxy fights.

Index-Based vs Active Management

p.59
  • Index-based investors track a benchmark; active managers try to beat it. Portfolios sit along an active management spectrum from index-tracking to concentrated few-stock portfolios.
  • Rationales for active: confidence in manager skill; client preference (manager must attract enough funds to cover active costs); client mandates such as ESG screens that force a more active approach.
  • Risks of active: reputation risk (rule or ethics violations), key person risk, and higher turnover creating higher tax burdens.

Benchmarks

p.60
  • A good equity benchmark is rules-based (consistent, objective, predictable), transparent (public, understandable), and investable (replicable). Providers limit stock-migration churn with buffering (ranges around breakpoints) and packeting (moving only part of a position at a time).
  • Market exposure dimensions: segment (broad vs focused, domestic vs international, developed / emerging / frontier), capitalization, growth vs value, other factors (momentum, liquidity, volatility, quality). Indexes range from exhaustive to selective.

Benchmark Weighting

p.61
  • Market-cap (usually free-float): each stock at its share of index market cap; the CAPM market portfolio; reflects strategy capacity.
  • Equal weighting: cuts concentration risk, slow to shift sector exposure, needs regular rebalancing, overweights small illiquid stocks.
  • Price weighting: like holding one share of each stock; high-priced stocks dominate; rarely an actual strategy.
  • Fundamental weighting by sales, income, dividends; bets that prices revert to fundamental value.

Benchmark Concentration

p.62
  • Effective number of stocks = the size of an equally weighted portfolio with the same concentration = 1 / HHI, where HHI is the sum of squared weights.
HHI = Σ wi2 effective stocks = 1 / HHI example: 500-stock index, HHI 0.01 → effective stocks = 100
  • Ongoing index adjustments: rebalancing (weight updates for market-cap changes) and reconstitution (swapping stocks that no longer fit, e.g. small-cap grown to mid-cap). Both create turnover and trading costs.

Overview of Fixed-Income Portfolio Management

Role of Fixed Income, and Liquidity

p.64
  • FI in the portfolio: (1) reduces risk through low volatility and correlation, (2) provides regular cash flow, (3) inflation-linked and floating-rate bonds hedge inflation risk.
  • Individual bonds can be very illiquid: many issuers with many issues and diverse features; issues drift into buy-and-hold hands; regulation plus thinner dealer inventories widen bid-ask spreads.
    • Most liquid: recent (on-the-run), high-quality sovereign debt. Least liquid: older (off-the-run), low-quality, smaller corporate debt.

Fixed-Income Strategies

p.65
  • Asset-liability management. Liability-driven investing (LDI) manages assets against liability characteristics (life insurers, DB plans); asset-driven liabilities (ADL) manages liabilities against asset characteristics.
  • Mandates may require ESG factors in the process.
  • Total return mandates vs a benchmark: pure indexing (replication, zero active risk); enhanced indexing (small deviations, duration kept close, modest alpha, slightly higher turnover); active (larger risk-factor mismatches, higher turnover, costs, fees).
  • Investor tax status (taxable vs exempt) shapes decisions depending on the jurisdiction's income and capital gains rules.

The Duration Family Tree

p.66
  • Macaulay duration: PV-weighted average time to cash flows. Below maturity for coupon bonds; floaters use half the coupon reset period.
  • Modified duration: MacDur / (1 + periodic rate) = % price change per 1% rate change.
  • Money duration: value change for a given rate change; PVBP / BPV uses a 0.0001 change.
  • Effective duration: for uncertain cash flows (embedded options).
  • Key rate duration: sensitivity to specific curve points.
  • Empirical duration: regression of historical price vs rate changes.
  • Spread duration: % value change if spread changes.
  • Duration times spread (DTS): useful when spreads move in proportion to their size.

Convexity

p.67
  • Convexity is the curvature of the price-yield relationship. Higher positive convexity means higher expected returns when rates move; for similar-duration bonds, expect higher convexity to come with lower YTM. Effective convexity uses a pricing model, like EffDur.
  • Convexity is roughly proportional to duration squared. For a given duration, more dispersed cash flows mean higher convexity, so a zero-coupon bond always has the lowest convexity for its duration.
  • Convexity likely matters most to returns when added via derivatives.

Portfolio Risk Measures; Alternatives to Direct Bonds

p.68
  • Portfolio duration and convexity = market-value-weighted averages of the bonds'. Adjust duration or spread duration to express rate and spread views.
  • Alternatives to holding bonds directly: bond ETFs and mutual funds; exchange-traded derivatives; OTC derivatives.
    • Total return swap (as receiver): likely lowest initial outlay and transaction costs, good access to illiquid or administratively complex market subsets, but you carry the payer's credit risk and regulation has raised costs.

Components of Return, and Leverage

p.69
  • Expected return decomposes into: (1) yield income = coupon / price; (2) rolldown return = projected ending price / beginning price, minus 1, assuming an unchanged curve (1 + 2 = rolling yield); (3) predicted price change from duration, convexity and benchmark rate views; (4) predicted price change from spread views; (5) projected currency gain or loss.
%Δvalue ≈ (−D × Δy) + (½ × C × Δy²) leverage: rp = ri + (VB/VE) × (ri − rB)
  • If asset return ri is below borrowing cost rB, leverage drags the portfolio below its unlevered return. Falling collateral values can force liquidations (fire sales).
  • Repos are explicit leverage using assets as collateral; securities lending has repo form but the motive is borrowing or lending the collateral itself. Rebate rate = collateral earnings rate minus security lending rate. Futures or swaps can create the same return effect.

Cash-Flow Matching

p.71
  • Often a better use of cash than buying back liabilities; may allow accounting defeasance (liabilities and the dedicated portfolio come off the balance sheet), improving the credit rating.
  1. Start with the longest-maturity liability.
  2. Find a bond maturing just before it.
  3. Buy the par amount such that par + final coupon covers the liability, net of coupons from bonds already bought that land just before it.
  4. Move to the previous liability and repeat; stop when none remain.
  • Securities are not sold to meet liabilities (unlike duration matching). Reinvestment risk on interim cash flows can arise, especially with annuity-shaped liabilities.

Bullet, Ladder, Barbell

p.72
  • Same total duration, different cash-flow placement along the curve:
    • Bullet: concentrated in the middle. Maximum yield when the curve is concave.
    • Ladder: even rungs across maturities. Natural periodic liquidity without selling; diversifies across the curve and between price and reinvestment risk.
    • Barbell: short and long ends only. Maximum dispersion and convexity.
  • Fixed-maturity corporate bond ETFs can build a ladder. A bond mutual fund offers more diversified default risk, lower transaction costs, and easier full liquidation.

Asset Allocation to Alternative Investments

The Alternative Asset Classes

p.74
  • Alternatives can raise return, lower risk, lift the Sharpe ratio, and bring low correlations.
  • Hedge funds: low correlation with other assets; may reduce equity beta.
  • Private equity: may specialize (health care, VC); higher returns but limited diversification benefit.
  • Private credit: direct lending (mainly income-producing property; illiquid vs public debt) and distressed debt (risk-return close to equities).
  • Commercial real estate: main benefit is the inflation hedge; core strategies target rental income, opportunistic target capital value.
  • Real assets: commodities, farmland (commodity-like or real-estate-like), timber, energy, infrastructure. Inflation hedge; infrastructure gives stable, modestly growing income; commodities are a distinct alpha source and some act as safe havens.

Suitability of Alternatives

p.75
  • Short horizon: alternatives look low-volatility and low-correlation, but the statistics are biased by smoothing, survivorship and backfill bias. Bonds are the better volatility reducer for an equity portfolio.
  • Long horizon: the main risk is missing the minimum required return; bonds may increase that risk, and alternatives diversify better given their low correlation with equities.
  • Also weigh liquidity, tax, and costs (fees are significant, e.g. 2-and-20 for hedge funds). Short data history makes return estimates hard; MVO struggles because returns are non-normal (illiquidity, optionality, potential low or negative returns).
  • Suitable for: large investors with selection expertise, horizons over 15 years (unless redemption is easy), strong governance, comfort with low transparency. Not suitable for investors who believe markets are price efficient.

Approaches to Asset Classification

p.76
  • Traditional: tends to overstate diversification and obscure the primary risk drivers.
  • Liquidity-based: rank within groups from more to less liquid. Fixed income: cash, bonds, then private credit. Equity: public equity, equity hedge funds, then private equity. Other: commodity futures and REITs, then private real estate and private real assets.
  • Economic environment-based: map assets to growth and inflation regimes. Non-indexed bonds fit low growth and deflation; public and private equity and private credit fit high growth; indexed bonds fit high inflation with low growth; commodities and real assets fit high inflation with high growth.
  • Risk factor-based: estimate factor sensitivities statistically. Reveals risks shared across asset classes and enables an integrated risk framework, but choosing the number of factors is hard and sensitivities depend heavily on the lookback period.

Investment Vehicles

p.77
  • Limited partnership: for large investors who can evaluate managers and strategies; LPs provide capital, the GP manages. A fund of one is an LP structure with a single limited partner.
  • Fund-of-funds: for investors without expertise; invests across partnerships, may specialize; the drawback is a double fee layer.
  • Separately managed accounts: for large investors wanting favorable terms.
  • UCITS: gives smaller investors access; more regulated, so returns may be insufficient.

Liquidity Concerns

p.78
  • Hedge funds: lockups; new capital and redemptions only periodically (e.g. quarterly) or with penalties and notice.
  • PE, private credit, real estate, real assets carry more liquidity risk still:
    • Partnerships may close to new investors; capital calls can arrive any time in the calldown period; the GP need not call the full commitment yet charges management fees on all of it.
    • Redemptions usually not allowed; the secondary market for LP interests is very restricted.
    • Long positions are usually more liquid than shorts; illiquid holdings can be walled off from standard redemption terms (side pockets); creditors rank ahead of LPs, so leverage matters.

Asset Allocation Approaches for Alternatives

p.79
  • Test data for return smoothing (serial correlation) and unsmooth as needed; test and adjust for non-normality, skew and excess kurtosis.
  • Monte Carlo simulation can generate scenarios without assuming normality.
  • MVO tends to over-allocate to alternatives; constrain the alternative allocation or limit volatility or downside risk, and treat output as guideline, not prescription.
  • Risk factor-based optimization: model factor exposures and expected factor returns, constrain specific exposures, then convert optimized exposures back to an asset allocation. Factor exposures and volatilities may not be stable over time.

Monitoring Alternative Investments

p.81
  • Monitor against stated goals, not benchmarks: indexes can be mutually inconsistent, and goals keep allocations tied to objectives. Especially important for illiquid holdings.
  • Challenges: reporting time lags; IRR is manipulable through the timing of calls and distributions, so investors may prefer MOIC (value of underlying investments less distributions, divided by total invested capital).

Economic Balance Sheet and Net Worth

p.84
  • Human capital: PV of expected future labor income. Sum each year's projected nominal income times survival probability, discounted at the nominal risk-free rate plus a premium for income volatility.
  • Financial capital: all other explicit assets, tangible and intangible, including the vested retirement portfolio.
  • Liabilities include explicit debts plus the PV of planned future expenses and bequests. Net worth is the balancing surplus: HC + FC = liabilities + net worth.
  • Over a lifetime, financial capital rises with age while human capital declines toward retirement.
  • More comprehensive than the traditional balance sheet (which ignores HC and future needs); better for planning lifetime consumption and bequests, though HC and future needs are hard to estimate.

Wealth

p.85
  • Drivers of national income and wealth growth: technology lifting productivity; shifts toward free-market capitalism (breaking monopolies, deregulation); privatization; more business formation, especially entrepreneurship; real asset-price appreciation.
  • Wealth levels: mass-affluent and affluent (top decile); HNWI about $1m+; VHNWI about $5m+; UHNWI about $50m+, where family offices play a vital role.
  • Wealth inequality: the Gini coefficient runs 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality); on a Lorenz curve, more curvature means more inequality.

Stages of the Wealth Life Cycle

p.86
  • Education (0–25), then early career (25–35): marriage, children, home purchase, education saving; little room for retirement saving.
  • Career development (35–50): skills and progression, retirement planning, accumulation begins in earnest.
  • Peak accumulation (50–60): greatest accumulation, more career risk.
  • Preretirement (60–65): total wealth peaks, liabilities shrink.
  • Early retirement (65–75): live on pension income and the portfolio.
  • Late retirement (75+): leisure costs fall, uninsured health costs may rise; longevity risk.
  • Career development through preretirement is the critical planning phase; preretirement onward is deaccumulation and distribution. Age ranges indicative.

Returns and Risk for Individuals

p.87
  • Return: nominal vs real; compare hedging inflation with beating inflation.
  • Risk measures: return volatility and drawdown (peak to trough), both reducible by diversification; plus the risk of missing financial objectives, where inflation is critical.
  • Risk tolerance = ability and willingness; inverse of risk aversion; may differ per goal. Risk capacity is the ability part, based on wealth, income, horizon, liquidity needs, goal importance; more objective. Risk perception is subjective.
  • Questionnaires give a numeric score but are highly subjective, and question order and framing (relative vs absolute losses) skew answers. Conversations work better for higher-net-worth clients.

Client Goals

p.88
  • Planned: retirement, charitable giving, wealth transfer, specific purchases, dependents' education. Unplanned: uncertain amount and timing, e.g. property repairs, uninsured medical costs.
  • Manager's role: quantify goals (stay realistic when hard to pin down, e.g. retirement), prioritize (reconcile conflicts; earlier does not mean more important), and change (reevaluate after material changes in circumstances).
  • Capital sufficiency analysis: are resources enough to achieve the goals, given risk tolerance?

Investment Policy Statement

p.90–92
  • Background: personal, financial, tax information.
  • Objectives: quantify where possible, reconcile competing goals, split primary vs secondary.
  • Investment parameters: asset-class preferences (risk-return and income profiles); risk tolerance (willingness and ability); liquidity preferences (cash reserve, limits on illiquids); time horizon ranges (over 15 years long, under 10 short; per objective); other preferences (legacy holdings, ESG); constraints (large unrealized gains, tax mitigation strategy).

Portfolio Management for Institutional Investors

Institutional Investors: Overview

p.94–95
Exam focusKnow the common characteristics vs individuals, the four allocation models (Norway, endowment, Canada, liability-driven), and each investor type's features, stakeholders, liabilities, horizon, risk tolerance, liquidity. In questions, specifics beat generalities: reason from principles (longer horizon and lower liquidity need mean greater risk tolerance). Be able to apply the leverage formulas.
  • Types: pension plans (DB, DC); charitable bodies (university endowments, private foundations); sovereign wealth funds; insurers (life; nonlife, focus P&C); banks.
  • IPS includes: return and risk tolerance; time horizon and liabilities; asset allocation policy (weights and ranges); rebalancing policy; reporting requirements; external constraints on allocations.

Common Characteristics vs Individuals

p.96
  • Scale. Smaller institutions (from about USD 25m) struggle to diversify and lean on external managers and consultants; larger ones (up to about USD 10b) access a wider universe but hit capacity-constrained strategies.
  • Long horizon: low liquidity needs (annual cash out may be about 5% of AUM).
  • Regulatory framework: location-dependent laws, accounting and tax; general push toward lower leverage, central clearing, reporting transparency.
  • Governance: formal structure (board, investment committee); CIO and investment office implement strategy.
  • Principal-agent conflicts: internal (committee and staff) and external (managers and fee structures, broker-dealers, consultants, board members).

Asset Allocation Models

p.97
  • Norway: passive, base case 60:40 equity:bonds, little or no alternatives, low tracking error and no outperformance potential, low cost.
  • Endowment: significant external active management, high alternatives allocation, outperformance potential, high cost.
  • Canada: endowment-like but more internal management.
  • Liability-driven: maximize expected surplus (assets minus liabilities) and manage surplus volatility.

Defined Benefit Plans

p.98–100
  • Features: contractual benefits (often a share of final salary); the sponsor contributes and bears investment risk (employees may contribute); mortality and longevity risks pool.
  • Stakeholders: sponsor (poor returns force contributions up, hitting shareholders), beneficiaries (sponsor default risk), staff, committee, board, government (tax incentives).
  • Liabilities: the PBO. Funded ratio = fair value of plan assets / PV of obligations. PBO rises with more years worked, higher salaries, longer life spans, lower turnover (vesting), lower expected return and discount rate, extra employee contributions.
  • Horizon lengthens (and risk tolerance rises) with a lower proportion of retired lives.
  • Risk tolerance falls with: underfunded status (negative surplus); weaker sponsor finances; higher correlation between plan assets and the sponsor's business; features that raise liquidity needs and shorten horizon (early retirement, lump-sum options); older or more retired workforces.
  • Liquidity needs rise with: older workers; higher retired-to-active ratio; a frozen plan (benefits exceed contributions); higher funded status (lower sponsor contributions likely); participants able to switch or withdraw.
  • External constraints: extensive fee and cost reporting; tax treatment shaping strategy; double-taxation relief internationally; accounting rules (funded status, valuation).
  • Objectives. Primary: meet the target return over the long horizon at risk consistent with the liabilities, asset growth matching liability growth, funded by returns and contributions. Secondary: minimize the PV of sponsor contributions.

Defined Contribution Plans

p.101–102
  • Stakeholders: sponsor carries no investment or longevity risk but has fiduciary duties (contribute, administer); beneficiaries bear investment and longevity risk; the board communicates and may set the default option; government provides tax incentives.
  • Liabilities: the required contributions. Horizon tied to participant age; default life-cycle (target-date) options either switch allocation with age per participant or pool cohorts with similar retirement dates.
  • Liquidity needs rise with older workers and switching or withdrawal rights.
  • External constraints: regulators often require participant education and default options; many plans are tax-deferred (taxed on withdrawal).
  • Objectives. Primary: prudently grow assets for retirement spending. Secondary, if active: beat the default option's passive SAA returns.
  • Allocation trend (DB and DC): from equity toward alternatives and fixed income to damp funded-ratio volatility; some home bias within equities.

Sovereign Wealth Funds

p.103–105
  • Types: budget stabilization (insulate from commodity prices and cycles); development (infrastructure, key industries); savings (nonrenewable revenues, future generations); reserve (returns on excess FX reserves); pension reserve (future pension liabilities).
  • Stakeholders: citizens current and future, investment offices, the SWF board (fiduciary duty), governments (returns help balance budgets).
  • Liabilities, horizon, liquidity: stabilization: uncertain liabilities, short horizon, highest liquidity need. Development: project-driven, medium to long term, low liquidity need. Savings: long term, low liquidity need. Reserve: target above the government bond yield, very long term, medium liquidity need. Pension reserve: long term; liquidity depends on accumulation (lower) vs decumulation (higher) phase.
  • External constraints: to resist political influence, need high-quality governance, independence, transparency, accountability. Generally tax-exempt; watch withholding taxes and treaties.
  • Objectives: stabilization: preserve capital, beat inflation with low loss probability, avoid assets correlated with government revenue. Development: support growth, earn above domestic GDP growth. Savings: maintain purchasing power while meeting budget needs. Reserve: beat the government bond yield. Pension reserve: fund future unfunded pension and social care payments.
  • Allocations: stabilization: defensive, mostly cash and FI. Development: mission-dependent. Savings: heavy equities and alternatives. Reserve: similar but fewer alternatives (liquidity). Pension reserve: heavy equities and alternatives during accumulation.

University Endowments

p.106–108

  • Liabilities: promised payouts under the spending policy; fundraising lowers the net spending rate.
  • Risk tolerance: usually high (low liquidity, perpetual horizon); lower when the university budget depends heavily on the endowment; higher with debt-issuing capacity.

Banks

p.111–113
  • Features: the bank earns the spread between deposits and loans; also safeguards assets, executes transactions, advises, invests. The ALMCo oversees investment activity.
  • Stakeholders: shareholders (profit), depositors and borrowers, creditors, rating agencies, regulators, communities; internally employees, managers, directors.
  • The core mismatch: liabilities are short-term and liquid (demand deposits needing no notice, term deposits, LT debt, trading payables, repo payables) while non-investment assets are long-term illiquid mortgages and commercial loans. Conclusion: high liquidity need, so investment portfolio assets stay very short term.
  • Objectives (primary): manage liquidity; reduce mismatches between non-investment assets and liabilities.

Insurance Companies

Trading Costs and Electronic Markets

Trading Costs

p.120
  • Explicit: direct, receipt-able costs: commissions, transaction taxes, stamp duty, exchange fees.
  • Implicit: bid-ask spread (inside spread = best ask minus best bid; midquote = their average); market impact (large trades move price); delay costs (slippage: prices move before the trade); opportunity costs (unrealized P&L on unexecuted trades).
  • Implicit costs are estimated by comparing trade price to a benchmark: effective spread, implementation shortfall, VWAP.

Effective Spread and Implementation Shortfall

p.121
effective spread (buy) = 2 × (trade price − midquote at trade) effective spread (sell) = 2 × (midquote − trade price) cost estimate = trade size × effective spread / 2 price improvement: effective spread < quoted spread
  • Effective spread misses market impact when an order is split and each piece measured separately, and cannot capture delay or opportunity costs.
  • Implementation shortfall: compares the actual portfolio against a paper portfolio built at the decision price (usually the midquote when the decision was made) at full desired size. Captures all implicit and explicit costs.

VWAP

p.122
  • Trade VWAP = money value of your trades from order start to completion / volume traded. VWAP benchmark = money value of the whole day's trades in the stock / volume.
cost (buy) = trade size × (trade VWAP − VWAP benchmark) cost (sell) = trade size × (VWAP benchmark − trade VWAP)
  • Easy to read, but problematic when your trades are a large fraction of benchmark volume or traded at the same rate as the market (bias toward zero).

Electronic Trading and Market Structure

p.123
  • Electronic order-driven markets bring lower costs (no floor, no exchange officials), precise rule enforcement, audit trails, genuinely hidden orders, and continuous weatherproof trading.
  • Computers dominate implementation: infinite attention span and scope, fast, disciplined, retain everything needed.
  • Fragmentation: one instrument trades across exchanges, ATSs and dealers (ATSs work like exchanges without regulatory authority beyond trading conduct; also called ECNs or MTFs). Smart order routing and liquidity aggregators knit venues together.

Electronic Trader Types; Speed

p.124–125
  • News traders; dealers (cut inventory instantly by taking liquidity when prices may move against them; avoid large positions in active stocks); arbitrageurs (price discrepancies across markets, taking or offering liquidity); front runners (low-latency AI spotting large or clustered same-side orders); quote matchers (exploit the option value of standing limit orders as exits).
  • HFT vs low latency: HFTs buy then sell rapidly; low-latency traders (often parasitic) may hold for a day or more. Latency = time from event to dependent action.
  • Why speed pays: take opportunities first, make markets first, cancel stale orders fast. Latency arises in learning of the event, computing the response, and delivering the instruction.
  • Fast communication: colocation in the exchange server room, microwave over fiber, minimal routers and switches, high-speed feeds. Fast computation: fast machines, optimized code (coding speed can matter more than execution speed), contingency tables of prearranged actions.

Electronic Brokers, Algos

p.126
  • Advanced orders: limit prices that track market conditions; if the exchange lacks the order type, the broker adjusts rapidly (subject to latency).
  • Tactics: multi-order routines, e.g. pinging with IOC orders to find hidden limit orders.
  • Algorithms: programmed fill strategies, e.g. splitting large orders to cut price impact, with transaction-cost models steering. VWAP algos participate in an equal fraction of volume through the interval, forecasting from historical and live volume, choosing limit vs market orders while minimizing visibility.

Risks of Electronic Trading; Abusive Practices

p.127–128
  • Risks: runaway algorithms (programming errors); fat-finger errors amplified by speed; overlarge orders demanding more liquidity than exists (2010 Flash Crash); malevolent order streams (manipulators, disgruntled employees, terrorists, denial-of-service floods); machine-learning models failing in extraordinary episodes.
  • Solutions: thorough software testing and mock sessions; market access controls; only authorized developers change code, with a second developer vouching; real-time order surveillance against preset parameters by traders and exchanges; brokers surveil client orders (no sponsored naked access); exchange price limits and trading halts.
  • Abusive practices. Front running takes liquidity from those front-run (legal if based on market data feed analysis). Market manipulation is generally illegal but hard to prove: trading for impact, rumormongering, wash trading across controlled accounts, spoofing/layering (dummy exposed orders with hidden real ones, at the risk the dummies fill), bluffing (pump and dump; defend with independent valuations), gunning the market (force stop-losses then buy back cheap), squeezing and cornering (control the resources needed to settle contracts).

Portfolio Performance Evaluation

Performance Evaluation Framework

p.134
  • Measurement: compute portfolio return vs benchmark. Attribution: find the sources of return and risk. Appraisal: skill or luck, and is it repeatable?
  • Attribution approaches: returns-based (regression, no holdings data needed; use for hedge funds); holdings-based (daily / weekly / monthly data, shorter intervals better, longer periods link shorter attributions; ignores transactions so may not reconcile to actual return); transactions-based (most accurate, most work).
  • Arithmetic vs geometric excess: arithmetic is R − B; geometric is (R − B)/(1 + B). Geometric handles multiperiod attribution without smoothing; arithmetic is easier for clients unless they are practitioners.

Brinson-Fachler Attribution

p.135
allocation = Σ (wactual − wBM) × (RBM,sector − RBM,total) selection = Σ wBM × (Ractual,sector − RBM,sector) interaction = Σ (wactual − wBM) × (Ractual,sector − RBM,sector)
  • Allocation measures the effect of weights deviating from policy; selection measures sector returns deviating from benchmark at benchmark weights; interaction is effectively the balancing figure so the three sum to the total active return.
  • Brinson-Hood-Beebower omits the subtraction of the total benchmark return in the allocation term: total allocation is unchanged but sector-level values differ. Use BF unless a question says BHB.

Carhart Four-Factor Model

p.136
Rp − Rf = ap + bp1RMRF + bp2SMB + bp3HML + bp4WML + Ep
  • ap = alpha, return above what systematic risk explains (assuming the factors capture it all); b = factor sensitivities (the bracketed factor terms are the tilt return); E = unexplained error.
  • RMRF = value-weighted index return minus 1-month T-bills. SMB = small minus big (size): average of three small-cap portfolios minus three large-cap. HML = high minus low (value): two high book-to-market portfolios minus two low. WML = winners minus losers (momentum): last year's winners minus losers.

Fixed-Income Attribution

p.137–138
  • Three methods; the exam asks for interpretation, not calculation: exposure decomposition (duration based), yield curve decomposition (duration based), yield curve decomposition (full repricing).
  • Exposure decomposition (sums to active return): interest rate allocation = duration effect (skill in duration bets) + curve effect (convexity, curve-shape calls); sector allocation (over- and underweights); bond selection (mispriced bonds, nonbenchmark holdings and weights).
  • Yield curve decomposition, duration based (sums to active return): yield (sector and maturity differences); roll (roll return vs benchmark); shift (overall duration difference); slope (flattening or steepening differential); curvature (reshaping differential); spread (sector spread changes); specific (bond-specific spread changes); residual (whatever duration and convexity miss).

Macro and Micro Attribution

p.140
  • Macro: the sponsor's decisions: allocations across portfolio managers and their returns vs manager benchmarks. Micro: each manager's decisions vs their own benchmark.
  • Both can use the Brinson decomposition (allocation + selection + interaction): macro over managers or styles, micro over segments (substyles, countries, sectors).

Benchmark Selection

p.141–142
  • Common benchmarks: absolute or target return; manager universe / peer group (these two typically fail the validity tests); broad market index; style indexes; factor-model based; returns-based; custom security based (usually via regression); liability based (pensions). Index and benchmark are not synonyms.
  • Valid benchmark properties (SAMURAI): Specified in advance, Appropriate, Measurable, Unambiguous, Reflective of the manager's opinions, Accountable (manager owns it), Investable.
  • Quality tests. Decompose P = M + S + A: S = style return = B − M; A = active management = P − B; total value added E = P − M. With a broad index as benchmark, S is assumed zero, so real style differences hide inside A.
    • A good benchmark shows correlation of A with S near zero (active success should not depend on the style being in favor) and significantly positive correlation of S with E (portfolio and benchmark should both beat the market when the style is in favor).
  • Misfit: if the wrong benchmark X is used when B fits the manager's normal portfolio, apparent active return P − X = true active return (P − B) + misfit return (B − X).

Benchmarking Alternatives

p.143–144
  • Hedge funds: all three candidates fail. Broad indexes: strategies too varied, leverage and derivatives, illiquidity, low transparency, near-zero correlation. Risk-free plus spread: hedge funds carry systematic risk and do not track the risk-free rate. Peer universes: characteristics rarely match a specific fund, plus backfill and survivorship bias and smoothing that lowers reported sigma.
  • Real estate: sample-built indexes biased to larger deals, appraisal smoothing, inconsistent leverage, unrealistic index assumptions (no transaction costs, normal liquidity).
  • Private equity: IRR standard but flawed (early gains and losses bias it); valuation methods differ so comparability suffers; portfolio companies sit at different stages; PME methodology possible but requires the right public proxy.
  • Commodities: benchmarks track futures, not assets: large gaps vs actual portfolios. Managed derivatives: no market indexes, and bespoke benchmarks may not fit. Distressed securities: nearly impossible: illiquid, unmarketable, stale pricing.

Appraisal Ratios

p.145–146

  • Sharpe and Treynor penalize upside and downside deviation alike. The information ratio generalizes Sharpe with a risky benchmark replacing the risk-free asset.
  • Sortino penalizes only deviation below the minimum acceptable return (e.g. set MAR = expected inflation for real capital preservation); better when returns are asymmetric.
  • Capture ratio measures return asymmetry (like convexity): above 1 = convex profile (preferable), below 1 = concave.

Capture and Drawdown

p.147
CR = UC / DC UC = manager return / benchmark return, when benchmark positive DC = manager return / benchmark return, when benchmark negative max drawdown % = peak-to-trough loss / peak value
  • UC above 100% and DC below 100% generally suggest outperformance. Curriculum examples use geometric averages of period returns.
  • Drawdown = cumulative peak-to-trough loss; duration runs from drawdown start until the cumulative loss recovers to zero (drawdown + recovery). Drawdowns expose bad strategies, weak controls, operational problems.
  • A capture ratio above 1, or avoiding large drawdowns (especially when the market is not strongly trending up), raises risk-adjusted returns, since recovering a loss takes a larger percentage gain (e.g. low-beta strategies). Especially valuable with short horizons and low risk tolerance.

Investment Manager Selection

Due Diligence Framework

p.149
  • Understand the sources of past returns, then estimate the probability of the same process delivering equal or better results.
  • Manager universe: screen out managers unsuitable for the IPS; check style fit (analysis must be meaningful, accurate, consistent, timely) and active vs passive stance.
  • Quantitative: past returns via attribution and appraisal (skill?), capture ratios, drawdowns.
  • Qualitative: investment due diligence (philosophy, process, people, portfolio construction) and operational due diligence (procedures, firm health and commitment, vehicle suitability, terms, monitoring).

Style Analysis

p.150
  • Returns-based (top down): regress returns on style indexes. Works for complex strategies, objective (immune to window dressing), data cheap and timely; but imprecise: an average over the window, not current or future exposure.
  • Holdings-based (bottom up): aggregate each stock's attributes. Point-in-time accuracy, but effort grows with complexity, depends on manager-provided data with lags, and window dressing is possible.
  • Both identify return drivers and risk factors and compare across managers and time; both are distorted by stale prices understating risk.

Team Behavioral Biases

p.151
  • Teams can damp individual biases (overconfident forecasts) but introduce their own; research on team benefit is mixed.
  • Team biases: groupthink (conflict avoidance beats honest expression; closed-mindedness, confirmation bias); authority bias (deferring to the expert defeats the point of a team); aversion to complexity (bike-shedding trivial issues).
  • Mitigation questions: team size (3–5 optimal per research); how decisions are made (secret ballot may help); how meetings run (agenda agreed in advance, important topics first); team diversity (social and cognitive).

  • Type I (hire or keep a manager who then underperforms): explicit cost, psychologically more painful, easy to measure, transparent to investors. Type II (pass on or fire one who then performs): opportunity cost, less painful, hard to measure.
  • Prevention: track the later performance of managers held, evaluated-but-rejected, and fired; ask what differentiates them, what drove the decisions, whether the factors match the stated philosophy, and what value retain/fire decisions added. Investment Due Diligence: Philosophy and Process

p.154–155
  • Philosophy: concise and clear, credible and consistent. Efficient-markets believers run passive strategies and earn risk premiums; inefficiency believers go active. Behavioral inefficiencies are short-lived (exploit fast); structural ones persist (less urgency). Ask: repeatable? capacity to exploit, covering costs and fees? minimum and maximum asset levels?
  • Signal creation: where does the edge come from and how is it retained: unique information, timing, or different interpretation?
  • Signal capture: how an idea becomes a position; repeatable and consistent with assumptions; who has final approval?
  • Portfolio construction: which securities; allocations consistent with philosophy and views; characteristics drifting as AUM grows; hard or soft stop losses and hedges; how longs and shorts are expressed (pairs); liquidity assessment and the count of illiquid names.
  • Monitoring: external factors (economy, markets) and internal (returns, risk, allocations); watch for process deviations such as style drift.

Vehicles: Pooled vs SMA

p.156
  • Pooled: everyone in one portfolio, no customization (mutual funds, ETFs, hedge funds).
  • SMA: the investor's own account. Versus pooled: higher costs (fixed costs unshared, trades unaggregated); direct legal ownership and control; customization, at the price of tracking risk from client constraints and micromanagement risk (trend chasing, avoiding unfamiliar assets and hedges); tax efficiency on capital gains; separate reporting with instant transparency.
  • Liquidity ranking: ETFs and closed-end funds most liquid; open-end slightly worse; LPs depend on terms; SMAs depend on the underlying assets.

Manager Fees

p.157
  • AUM-based: rewards asset gathering, added value, and rising markets alike; the aim is to pay for skill, not luck (especially short term); the ad valorem percentage might fall as markets rise; very large managers may stop taking risk.
  • Performance-based aligns interests; three forms, often with high-water marks and clawbacks:
    1. Symmetrical: base plus a share of performance, full upside and downside.
    2. Bonus, full upside, limited downside: greater of base, or base plus share of positive performance.
    3. Bonus capped both ways: as (2) but up to a limit.
  • Forms 2 and 3 pay the base even in underperformance: economically the manager holds a call option on the active return (strike at the base fee; form 3 adds a short call at the cap). Danger: option value grows with risk, tempting managers to raise portfolio risk.

Global Investment Performance Standards (GIPS)

GIPS vs Standards of Professional Conduct

p.159
  • The SPC is mandatory and applies to individuals. GIPS is voluntary (encouraged) and applies to firms, governing calculation and presentation of returns to prospective clients and investors; it also covers asset owners competing for business. Eight sections; the first three dominate the exam.
  • The two are fully consistent, but adopting GIPS requires policies beyond the SPC.
  • Terminology: segregated account = one client's portfolio (SMA); pooled fund = multiple investors, either broad distribution (public, e.g. mutual funds) or limited distribution (private, e.g. hedge funds).

The Firm, and Discretion

p.160
  • GIPS applies firm-wide, never per composite, fund, or portfolio. A firm is an entity marketed as a distinct business: organizationally or functionally separate, with discretion over assets and autonomy over the investment process.
  • The firm needs a clear written definition of discretion, applied consistently; a portfolio is discretionary if the firm can implement the intended strategy (client risk preferences alone do not make it nondiscretionary).
  • Total firm assets = aggregate fair value, discretionary or not, fee-paying or not, including subadvised assets when the firm picks the subadvisor; excluding advisory-only assets and uncalled committed capital. Nondiscretionary accounts and funds must stay out of composite and pooled fund assets.

Fundamentals of Compliance; Portability

p.161–162
  • Initial compliance claim needs at least five compliant years (or since inception if younger). Firms must document GIPS policies; obey law; never present false or misleading performance anywhere; give prospects lists of appropriate composites and funds and make every reasonable effort to deliver a relevant GIPS report, refreshed after 12 months for continuing prospects; use total-return benchmarks matching the strategy; correct material errors and redistribute; keep supporting records; and follow the rules for using or linking another firm's performance.
  • Portability: past performance may link to the new or acquiring firm only if substantially all decision makers move, the process stays substantially intact and independent, records support the track record, and there is no break in it. A new limited-distribution fund with no record should show a GIPS report for a composite or fund run in the same or similar strategy.

Return Rules

p.163
  • Total returns, trade-date accounting, accrual accounting. Sub-year returns must not be annualized. Cash and equivalents count in all returns even if the firm does not control the cash investment.
  • Returns come after transaction costs (commissions, exchange fees, taxes, spreads; not custody fees); estimate only when actuals are unknown. If costs cannot be split from a bundled fee, deduct the whole bundle or the portion containing transaction costs (plus management fees for net-of-fee returns).
  • Never link actual and theoretical performance.
  • Time-weighted returns required, except money-weighted may be shown when the firm controls external cash flows AND the portfolios are either significantly illiquid by strategy or closed-end / fixed-life / fixed-commitment. Then present since-inception MWR at least annually using daily external cash flows.

p.164–165
  • Value monthly and compute TWR at least monthly (month end or last business day); pooled funds outside composites: annually; private market portfolios: quarterly.
  • Without daily returns, value and cut a subperiod return at every large external cash flow: the firm defines large (absolute or percentage), documents it, and follows it without exception; all pooled fund subscriptions and redemptions count as large. Subperiod returns link geometrically.

Valuation Requirements

p.166
  • Fair value required: the objective, observable, unadjusted quoted price (with accrued interest) for an identical asset in an active market on the measurement date. If unavailable, prefer in order: (1) similar assets in active markets; (2) similar assets in inactive markets; (3) observable market inputs other than prices (P/E, dividend yield); (4) subjective unobservable inputs (DCF).

Composites: Construction

p.167–169
  • A composite aggregates portfolios with similar mandates, objectives, or strategies. All discretionary fee-paying segregated accounts belong in at least one composite; discretionary fee-paying pooled funds join every composite whose definition they meet. A strategy offered both as fund and segregated account puts the fund in that composite; a fund-only strategy needs no composite. Nondiscretionary, simulated, and model portfolios are excluded (model info can be clearly labeled supplemental). A composite must include every portfolio meeting its definition; non-fee-paying discretionary portfolios may be included. Definition changes are never retroactive.
  • Composite returns, three methods: (1) weight portfolio returns by beginning values; (2) weight by beginning values plus modified-Dietz-weighted external cash flows; (3) aggregate method: treat the composite as one mega portfolio and compute modified Dietz.
  • New portfolios join on a timely, consistent, composite-specific schedule; only portfolios managed for the full measurement period count. Terminated portfolios stay in history through their last full period under discretion. Portfolios move between composites only on documented client-directed mandate changes or composite redefinition, with history staying in the original composite. Portfolios below a composite's minimum asset level stay out. Significant cash flows that impede timely implementation: temporarily exclude the portfolio, or use a temporary new account.

Composites: Reporting

p.170–171
  • TWR composite reports must show: at least 5 years of annual performance (or since inception), building to at least 10; composite and benchmark returns for all years, using total-return (never price-only) benchmarks matching the strategy, with components, weights, and methodology disclosed for custom blends, or an explanation if no benchmark exists; composite assets and total firm assets at each period end; and, where monthly returns exist, the three-year annualized ex post standard deviation of composite and benchmark at each annual period end.
  • With six or more portfolios at period end, also report the number of portfolios and an internal dispersion measure for each year (how consistently the strategy was implemented): high-low, interquartile range, equal- or asset-weighted standard deviation, or any fair and accurate method.

Verification

p.172
  • An independent qualified third party audits the firm's GIPS process on a sample basis. Recommended, not required.
  • Verification gives assurance that policies for composite and pooled fund maintenance and for calculating, presenting, and distributing performance were designed in compliance and implemented firm-wide. It does not assure all data is accurate.
  • Verifiers check recordkeeping, policies and procedures, the firm definition, completeness of composite lists and limited-distribution fund descriptions, and the total firm assets calculation. Options Basics: Calls and Puts

p.177–178
  • Value = worth of the position at expiry (intrinsic), before initial cost. Profit = value minus net initial outlay (add any net premium received); simple accounting, no time value of money. Payoff diagrams plot against the stock price at expiration.
  • Long call: zero value below strike (OTM), rises one-for-one above (value = stock − strike). Max loss = premium; breakeven = strike + premium.
  • Long put: value = strike − stock below strike, zero above. Breakeven = strike − premium.
  • Shorts mirror longs: value to short = −value to long; profit to long = loss to short; breakevens identical for long and short of the same position.
  • Long call + short put (same underlying, expiry, strike) = synthetic long forward.

Protective Put

p.179
  • Long stock + long put: buying downside protection, put strike usually below the current price. Losses hedged below the strike; upside kept.
breakeven = initial stock price + premium max loss = breakeven − strike max profit = unlimited

Covered Call

p.180
  • Long stock + short call: selling off upside, call strike usually above the current price (the lower the strike, the likelier the stock gets called away). Gains hedged above the strike; premium cushions the downside.
breakeven = initial stock price − premium max profit = strike − breakeven max loss = breakeven

Collar

p.181
  • Long stock + long put (strike XL below spot) + short call (strike XH above spot): protected below XL like a protective put, upside surrendered above XH like a covered call. Net off the option premiums for profit.
breakeven = initial stock price − net premium max profit = XH − breakeven max loss = breakeven − XL
  • Strikes are commonly set so premiums cancel: a zero-cost collar.

Straddle

p.182
  • Long straddle: buy call and put, same strike and expiry. V-shaped value around the strike; max loss = total premium when both expire OTM. Ideal when you expect a large move but not its direction.
  • Short straddle: sell both; inverted-V profit peaking at the strike.
  • Breakeven where the ITM option's value equals total premium: strike ± total premium.

Bull and Bear Spreads

p.183–184
  • Bull spread (calls or puts, same expiry): buy the low strike XL, sell the high strike XH.
    • Bull call spread: net premium paid (debit spread). Value flat at zero below XL, rises between strikes, flat above XH (both ITM, changes hedged).
    • Bull put spread: net premium received (credit spread). Same profit shape.
  • Bear spread: sell low strike, buy high strike: the short of the equivalent bull spread. Bear call = credit; bear put = debit. Profit shapes identical for call and put versions.
  • Use when expectations for the stock are only limited bullish or bearish.

The Greeks

p.185
  • Delta: value change per +1 in the underlying. Positive for calls, negative for puts; |delta| runs 0 (deep OTM) to 1 (deep ITM), about 0.5 ATM. The underlying itself (or its forward or future) has delta 1. Delta is the slope of the value curve.
  • Theta: daily value change from time passing; negative for long calls and puts.
  • Vega: value change per +1% volatility; positive for long calls and puts.
  • Gamma: delta change per +1 in the underlying; positive for long options; greatest ATM near expiry. Gamma is the curvature.
  • All hold with other factors constant.

Position Deltas and Delta Hedging

p.186
  • Net position delta = sum of constituent deltas, signs included. Covered call: 1 − call delta, so as the stock rises the call goes ITM and net delta approaches zero (upside progressively hedged). Protective put: 1 − |put delta|, so as the stock falls the put goes ITM and net delta approaches zero (downside progressively hedged).
  • Delta-neutral constructions: underlying + opposite forward or future on the same quantity; underlying + 1/|put delta| puts (same side); underlying + 1/|call delta| calls (opposite side).

Calendar Spreads

p.187
  • Calls or puts, same strike; calls when long-term bullish, puts when bearish.
  • Long calendar: buy longer-dated, sell shorter-dated. Use near-ATM options (longer-dated has lower theta). Net debit, since the longer option holds more time value. Held to the near expiry: position value = remaining time value in the long option (intrinsic values cancel, the expiring option has no time value); profit if that exceeds the net premium. Best near ATM with high volatility; worst case loses the net premium.
  • Short calendar: sell longer-dated, buy shorter-dated; away-from-ATM options (longer-dated there can carry higher theta); credit. Benefits from a big underlying move or falling implied volatility.

Volatility Skew and Smile

p.188
  • For a given expiry: a smile means higher implied vols further from ATM; a skew (more common) means higher implied vol for OTM puts and lower for OTM calls.
  • A sharp rise in skew plus surging implied volatility signals bearish sentiment: OTM puts bid up as insurance, OTM calls unwanted.
  • Trading anomalous skew: if put implied vol looks too rich vs calls, buy the OTM call and sell the same-expiry OTM put: a long risk reversal. It carries positive delta, so delta-hedge by selling the right amount of the underlying.

Swaps, Forwards, and Futures Strategies

Interest Rate Swaps

p.190
  • Converting fixed to floating (or back) is equivalent to amending duration. Converting a floating loan to fixed cuts cash-flow volatility but raises market-value volatility (duration): the tradeoff always exists, and basis (spread) risk can spoil the result.
  • Positive duration loses when rates rise; negative duration loses when rates fall; |D| grows with how fixed the cash flows are.
notional: NS = ((MDT − MDP) / MDS) × MVP (T target, P portfolio) swap duration = D(receive leg) − D(pay leg) e.g. MDpay-fixed = MDfloating − MDfixed (negative: fixed D > FRN D)

Locking In a Short-Term Rate

p.191

  • Eurodollar future: quote = 100 − annualized yield on the reference 90-day deposit; BPV $25. Fear rising rates: quotes will fall, so sell futures (sell high, buy back cheap if rates rise).
  • Contracts for the hedge = loan principal / reference deposit size; this hedges interest over the reference period.

Currency Swaps, Forwards, Equity Swaps

p.193–194
  • Cross-currency basis swap: two currencies, notionals exchanged and returned at the initial spot rate, usually floating-for-floating; converts debt from one currency to another (variants without principal exchange exist). The payer of the basis pays the non-USD rate plus basis and receives the USD reference rate, having received non-USD and delivered USD at the initial exchange (reversed at maturity).
  • Forwards are the easiest hedge of future foreign-currency receipts or payments; futures work if maturity and size fit. A dollar fund hedges a future euro receipt by selling euros forward or selling euro futures.
  • Equity swaps: one party pays a variable series on a stock, basket, or index; the other pays a different equity or rate series, or fixed. A total return swap includes dividends. Careful: rate-based flows need day counts; price-change flows do not.

Asset Allocation Using Derivatives

p.195
  • Futures: (1) fix the absolute amounts (not %) to adjust; (2) sell contracts to fully hedge away the unwanted exposure; (3) buy contracts for the desired exposure.
  • Swaps: the amounts set the notionals; pay away the unwanted returns, receive the desired ones.
  • Cash-flow risk: if swap payments exceed receipts by more than the portfolio's cash generation, there is a problem, because capital gains only turn to cash on sale and the swap was chosen to avoid selling.

Trading Volatility

p.196–197
  • VIX = expected S&P 500 volatility over the next 30 days; trade via VIX futures and options on futures. Volatility correlates negatively with stock returns, so hedge equities with long VIX futures or long calls on futures (subsidize the premium by selling lower-strike VIX puts: a collar).
  • Variance swaps (OTC): buyer receives variance notional × (realized variance − strike variance) at expiry. Long variance is long gamma (payoff convex in volatility).

Currency Management

Currency Return and Risk

Strategic Currency Decisions

p.201
  • Passive matches benchmark exposures; discretionary allows small deviations for risk control; active allows larger deviations for value added. An overlay manager can implement any of them.
  • Favoring passive: higher risk aversion; shorter objectives; near-term income and liquidity needs; fixed-income assets; low hedging costs; skeptical clients or managers without views.
  • Active approaches: economic fundamentals (favorable: lower inflation, higher real and nominal rates, decreasing currency risk, long-term undervaluation); carry trade (borrow the low-rate currency, invest in the high-rate one, stay unhedged; does badly in crises when volatility spikes); volatility trading (long or short calls and puts together to trade vol direction); technical rules.

Currency Hints

p.202
  • Fix the currency in focus (B) and work in direct quotes P/B. Typical setup: a domestic investor in country P holds an asset in country B and is at risk if B depreciates.
  • Buying futures, forwards, or calls on B increases exposure to B; selling futures or forwards, or buying puts on B, decreases it. A call on B is a put on P and vice versa.
  • Futures adjust upside and downside symmetrically. Options are asymmetric and cost premium; cutting cost means giving up upside or accepting more downside. For analysis, ATM |delta| is about 0.5: OTM lower, ITM higher.

Hedging Mechanics and Roll Yield

p.203
  • Static hedge: set the initial term to the hedging period and leave it. Dynamic hedge: rebalance periodically: costlier but more accurate. Rolling shorter contracts is static between rollovers, dynamic at them.
  • Contango (FP/B > SP/B because iP > iB): B trades at a forward premium; F converges down to S. A hedger short F and long S earns positive roll yield, cheapening the hedge; a hedger long F and short S pays negative roll yield.
  • Backwardation (F < S because iP < iB): B at a forward discount; F converges up. The short-F hedger now earns negative roll yield, the long-F hedger positive.

Perfect, Cross, Macro, and Minimum-Variance Hedges

p.204
  • A perfect hedge held to expiry locks in the forward premium or discount, but may not exist and may count as a taxable sale. A cross hedge uses a correlated but different vehicle: riskier. A macro hedge targets portfolio-wide risk: inherently imperfect and cross.
  • Minimum-variance hedge ratio: regress the change in RDC (dependent) on the change in RFX (independent); the slope is the MVHR = foreign currency to trade / foreign currency exposure.
RDC = α + βRFX; β = ρ(RDC,RFX) × σ(RDC)/σ(RFX)
  • Single-currency intuition via ρ(RFC, RFX): negative correlation means currency moves partly hedge the asset, so MVHR < 1; positive means they reinforce, so MVHR > 1.

Forwards vs Options for Hedging

p.205–208
  • Forwards and futures: symmetric, kill upside and downside alike, no upfront premium; forwards are the standard currency hedge. Options: asymmetric, premium upfront; cheapening always trades away upside or accepts downside. All combinations build from six payoffs: long and short underlying, long call, long put, short call, short put.
  • Protective put on the currency: upside kept; the put offsets losses below its strike. ATM costs most, protects immediately; OTM costs less, protects less.
  • Put spread: protective put plus a further-OTM short put: protection holds only between the two put strikes, re-exposed below the short strike, premium income cuts cost.
  • Collar: long currency, buy OTM put, sell OTM call: floor below, cap above, cheaper.
  • Seagull: collar plus a further-OTM short put: two sold options cut cost further; protection is a band, re-exposed below the lowest strike, capped above the call.
  • Delta quoting: a 25-delta call has delta 0.25; a 75-delta put has delta −0.75 (put deltas are negative). ATM means 50-delta.

Emerging Market Currencies

p.209

  • Benchmarking is easier for public securities. The private investment life cycle: precommitment / commitment, capital deployment, capital distribution, exit. The J-curve: losses expected in early years.
  • Sources of diversification potential: distinct life-cycle phases (VC, pre-IPO growth equity); distinct value-creation processes (buyout restructuring); an investment life cycle independent of the general business cycle.

Structures

p.229
  • Private pooled funds are typically limited partnerships: LPs invest, the GP manages. Investment routes: indirect (fund investments, LP co-investments alongside the fund) and direct, managed by the LP (direct co-investments, e.g. angel investors).
  • Private structures are less standardized; debt is often more complex than public bonds (leveraged loans, mezzanine); new legal structures get created as needed.
  • SPEs facilitate building and operating real estate and infrastructure projects, entering concession agreements with public authorities.
  • Take-private: phase 1: form an acquisition company and raise short-term finance to buy the target's securities; phase 2: merge the two and raise long-term finance.

Return Metrics

p.230
IRR: rate setting NPV of cash flows to zero (assumes reinvestment at IRR) ROI = cash flows received / cash flows invested no interim flows over n years: ROI = (1 + IRR)n PIC = capital invested / committed capital TVPI = (distributions + NAV) / capital invested (= MOIC = MOM) DPI = distributions / capital invested RVPI = NAV / capital invested TVPI = DPI + RVPI
  • Uncertain capital calls make liquidity management vital for LPs: parking undrawn commitments in liquid assets lowers portfolio returns and muddies public vs private comparisons. Compare funds within the same vintage year.
  • PME: (1) assume the fund's cash flows were invested in the public market to get a public terminal value; (2) run the IRR on the cash flows with that terminal value as the final flow.
  • Pooled fund IRR: never average the underlying IRRs; pool all the cash flows and compute one IRR.

Private Risk and Return

p.231
  • Returns vary more across managers than in public markets. Along the J-curve: commitment phase earns liquid public returns on undrawn capital; deployment shows fees without returns (negative); distribution lifts IRR through zero (when returns equal contributed capital) toward the fund's final compound IRR. Liquidity planning is crucial: engage GPs regularly, respond to market conditions.
  • Private returns lean on long-term appreciation rather than income, and should exceed public returns to compensate for cash-flow uncertainty and illiquidity.
  • By class: equity: higher risk-adjusted returns via new companies and LBO leverage; diversification from pre-IPO access and GP value creation decoupled from the cycle. Debt: different issuer life-cycle stages, higher default risk, complex structures, higher illiquidity. Real estate: more development exposure, equity-like returns, less liquidity than REITs. Infrastructure: higher returns demanded for greenfield exposure, long horizons, high execution risk.

GP and Investor Perspectives; the Investment Process

GP Roles Across the Life Cycle

p.233
  • Precommitment: build the investment thesis from the GP's expertise. Commitment: raise capital, sign limited partnership agreements.
  • Deployment: finalize due diligence, negotiate and structure deals, draw down equity and invest, execute the value-creation plan, monitor, follow on as milestones hit, provide periodic valuations. GPs may use subscription lines of credit to time drawdowns.
  • Distribution and exit: structure exits, distribute to LPs. GPs may offer secondaries: for LPs these bring diversification, faster capital deployment, and liquidity for sellers.

Fees and Alignment

p.234
  • Management fees (1–2%) on committed capital; once deployed, on committed capital less the cumulative cost of exits and write-offs. Advisory fees possible for transactions benefiting the fund.
  • Carried interest (15–20%) usually above a hurdle; may carry a high-water mark (no carry unless NAV exceeds the previous hurdle-adjusted peak) and clawback provisions (early carry returned if later performance is poor).
  • Hard hurdle: carry only on profits above the hurdle. Soft hurdle: a catch-up rate (usually 100%) applies above the hurdle until the GP reaches full carry on total profits; at 100% catch-up, that happens when total profits = hurdle / (1 − carry split).

Private Equity

VC and Growth Equity; Convertible Preferred

p.238
  • Venture capital: minority equity to launch a new business. Growth equity: minority equity in a young company with big growth potential. Preseed comes from founders, family, friends; VC funds often invest alongside angels. Fund phases: drawdown (seed capital), investment (series / follow-on financing), returns harvesting (exits, distributions). Rule of thumb: 100 pitches, 10 due-diligenced, 1 investment.
  • Investments often use convertible preferred shares: liquidation preference value (LPV) = the multiple of initial proceeds CPS holders get without converting; conversion ratio = common shares per CPS. Holders convert when equity exceeds the LPV.
OCP per common share = LPV / (conversion ratio × number of CPS) OCP (total equity) = OCP per share × # common shares + LPV on exit: equity < LPV CPS take everything LPV < equity < OCP CPS take LPV, common take the excess equity > OCP CPS convert to common

Buyouts and LBO Mechanics

p.239
  • Buyout equity: controlling stakes in mature companies to reposition and unlock value. Opportunities: take-privates, corporate divestitures, private company sales. Dividend recapitalization: the company adds debt to pay existing shareholders a special dividend.
  • LBO mechanics: the financial sponsor creates an acquisition company. Step 1: it buys target shares with sponsor equity plus short-term borrowings and repays existing debt (change-of-control provisions force this). Step 2: acquirer and target merge, and the post-LBO company raises medium- to long-term capital to replace the bridge debt.
  • PE risks: liquidity, valuation, agency, operating and business, interest rate and leverage, macroeconomic, dilution.

PE Valuation

p.240

  • Down round: step-up below 1. A ratchet lowers exercise and conversion prices of options and CPS to compensate.
  • Growth equity: common equity follows the VC method; CPS follows the payoff relations above.

Leveraged Loans and High-Yield Bonds

p.243
  • High yield: callable at a premium after a lockout of several years; early retirement otherwise needs an expensive, refusable tender. Callable bond = straight bond − call option; valued on binomial trees, where the up rate = down rate × e2σ.
  • Z-spread: constant spread over Treasury spots. OAS: option-adjusted, leaving only credit and liquidity compensation.
  • Leveraged loans pay MRR + quoted margin (QM); investors require MRR + discount margin (DM). QM below DM: below par; equal: par; above: above par. Callable at par soon after issue. Low rate risk, but spread risk remains from the fixed QM.
effective duration = (PV− − PV+) / (2 × Δcurve × PV0)
  • Callables show negative convexity at low yields. Because benchmark rates and spreads move inversely, empirical duration runs below analytical duration.

Convertibles, Mezzanine, Unitranche

p.244
  • Convertible bond = straight bond + equity call. Conversion ratio = common shares per $1,000 par; conversion price = $1,000 / conversion ratio.
  • Mezzanine: subordinated with warrants or conversion rights, PIK interest common. Subordination is contractual (intercreditor agreement) or structural (lend to a holding company that owns equity in the borrower).
  • Unitranche: senior and subordinated combined into one issue at a single blended rate; an agreement among lenders sets the first-out (senior) and last-out (junior) tranches.

Debt Profiles, Risk and Return

p.245

  • Risks: credit, interest rate, liquidity, inflation, and reliance on direct-lending expertise.
  • Niches: specialty financing (credit cards, leasing); liquidity financing (short-term, money-market style).

Private Special Situations

Special Situations Overview

p.247–248
  • Short- and long-term opportunities exploiting financial dislocation: financial distress, and corporate-action or event-driven trades (merger arbitrage, capital structure arbitrage, convertible arbitrage).
  • Distressed debt: issuers likely unable to service debt; high insolvency (assets below debt) and bankruptcy odds. Strategies: short the stock of a soon-insolvent issuer; buy distressed debt expecting higher recovery; buy and profit through reorganization. Loss in default: credit loss rate = realized default rate × loss severity.
  • Distress markers: spread above 10%, loan under 90% of par, or stock near delisting. Corporate causes: high leverage, asset impairment or rising liabilities, weak operations, uncompetitive model, confidence or management crisis, macro and industry conditions. Sovereign causes: high fiscal deficits, inflexible monetary policy or exchange rates, adverse conditions; reserve-currency developed issuers carry less credit risk, foreign-currency debt more; sovereign immunity may block claims.
  • Risks to distressed strategies: macro and industry; idiosyncratic (fraud); credit, where expected loss = POD × LGD and in deep distress POD approaches 1 so EL approaches LGD; liquidity (delisting); operational (lost employees, customers, suppliers); legal (creditor disputes, bankruptcy costs); reputational (ESG duties, not exploiting vulnerable entities such as heavily indebted poor countries).

Financing Strategies and Bankruptcy

p.249
  • Prebankruptcy fixes. Liquidity shortfall: covenant changes, short-term credit extension, payment rescheduling. Long-term solvency gaps: asset sales, distressed debt exchange, secured debt or equity raise, debt-for-equity exchange.
  • Bankruptcy routes: free-fall; prepackaged (agreed before filing); debtor-in-possession financing; asset liquidation.
  • Process: petition filed; automatic stay on creditor claims (with relief exceptions); fraudulent conveyance provisions reach back up to 2 years in the US. Chapter 7: trustee liquidates. Reorganization: Chapter 11 (firms, partnerships), 15 (foreign issuers), 9 (municipalities); the company becomes debtor in possession with 120 days to file a plan; consent needs two-thirds by value and half by number per claimant class (a blocking position is one-third by value); then court approval and execution.
  • Bankruptcy, failure to pay, or restructuring is likely a credit event under a CDS, paying LGD × notional to the protection buyer.

Merger Arbitrage

p.251
  • Cash-for-stock: buy the target. Stock-for-stock: buy the target, short the acquirer at the deal ratio. Earn the arbitrage spread (announced price minus post-announcement market price) if the deal closes.
  • Risks: debt financing dries up; government or antitrust block; economic conditions turn; due diligence uncovers fraud; a third party counteroffers.
  • Debt angles: buy bonds before an announcement triggers change-of-control buybacks at a premium; or the acquirer tenders for the target's debt at a premium.

Convertible Arbitrage

p.254

  • Hedges: credit risk with bought CDS protection; interest rate risk with a pay-fixed swap; equity market risk with shorts or long puts.

Special Situations: Diligence, Valuation, Risk

p.255
  • Due diligence under event time pressure: ratio and rating screening; credit modeling with structural (BSM) and reduced-form (Altman Z-score) models; financial statement and competitive analysis; worst-case liquidation scenario; bankruptcy filings review; legal review of the debt profile, checking for voidable preference.
  • Valuation: income (DCF) with distressed adjustments: cash flows down, required returns up, terminal value invalid without going concern; multiples, only against distressed comparables unless recovery is the scenario; asset-based / NAV with a liquidation waterfall paying senior creditors first.   • Return sources: credit (expected losses below market-implied), liquidity (buying from forced sellers earns the premium), legal (skill in complex creditor negotiations).

Private Real Estate

Investment Features and Development

p.257
  • Risk spectrum: opportunistic (new development, mezzanine, distressed) and value-add (vacancies, upgrades and repositioning, high-yield CMBS) sit in private RE funds; then core-plus (minimal repositioning), core (stabilized income, public REITs), senior debt (mortgages, IG CMBS). Funds are open-end (interval) or closed-end.

Value Drivers and Portfolio Characteristics

p.258
  • Economic drivers: property specifics (location, age, amenities); macro (GDP, jobs, wages); regional and local conditions (business climate, infrastructure, regulation and tax, proximity of suppliers, contractors, employers); structural demand shifts (hybrid work).
  • RE cycle: recovery, expansion, recession and oversupply. Warning: multiyear projects planned in the expansion can complete right into recession and oversupply. Stabilized NOI = expected NOI once fully operational.
  • Portfolio traits: long horizon, illiquid; a secondary market in fund units is developing; open-end funds may gate withdrawals in crises.

  2. Property and project plan review: feasibility studies, reputation of the A&E consultants.
  3. Developer, contractor, supplier review: experience and financial strength; turnkey agreements concentrate execution risk.

Private RE Valuation

p.260

p.261

Cycle: planting and treatment (large outflows), management (small outflows), thinning (large inflow), harvest (largest inflow). Secondary income: selling carbon credits as offsets.

Infrastructure

Infrastructure Investment Features

p.263
  • Risk spectrum: opportunistic (greenfield, often developing markets); value-add (greenfield or brownfield, less proven markets); core-plus (brownfield / secondary stage, low construction risk); core (secondary-stage assets in established markets).
  • TICCS classification: business risk, industry, corporate governance, geoeconomic.
  • Unique features: a government authority grants concession agreements, provides or guarantees debt, or stabilizes operator revenues; assets are purpose-built and transfer to government for nothing at term end (terminal value zero); single projects run through SPVs/SPEs.
  • Revenue sources: commercial (merchant) payments; fixed availability-based payments; regulation-based payments (e.g. contracts for difference guaranteeing energy prices for renewables).
  • Investment traits: high cash yields; diversifying against the cycle (inelastic demand); liability-matching potential.

Infrastructure Diligence, Valuation, Risk

p.264
  • Due diligence: jurisdictional, legal, regulatory (including ESG); the engineering, procurement and construction contract; concession scope, specifications, term.
  • Valuation: DCF preferred. Raise the discount rate for larger size (less liquid), higher leverage (falls as debt repays), lower profitability, heavier capital investment (greenfield above brownfield), country risk, currency risk.
  • Risk and return: core projects suit long-term liability matching, especially availability-based or regulated fixed payments; inelastic demand diversifies against the cycle; inflation protection when revenues are inflation-linked.
  • Scenarios and sensitivities: base / worst / best cases; shock key inputs (e.g. forecast passenger numbers for a runway).
