Section 2 · Understanding Embodied Intelligence

EI has five building blocks. Three drive action. Two make it learnable.

An EI system is not one technology but five interdependent blocks. Understanding what each does, and what can go wrong in it, is the foundation for evaluating every company in the space.

👁️

SENSE

Cameras, tactile sensors, and force/torque feedback that perceive the world and build a continuously updating model of it.

🧠

THINK

AI models that reason about physical consequences, plan action sequences, and generate precise motor commands.

💪

ACT

Actuators, arms, grippers, end-effectors that execute physical commands and adapt in real time to force feedback.

📚

DATA

Real-world physical interaction data. Cannot be scraped from the internet. Must be collected one episode at a time in real environments.

🌐

SIMULATION

Virtual environments where robots practice at scale. Fast and cheap, but accuracy degrades sharply for deformable objects like fabric.

Inside the robot brain: what to test at each layer

SENSE — "What do I see?"

Fuses vision, touch, audio, language into one unified representation. Builds an updating internal picture of the scene including object positions, material properties, spatial relationships.

  • Multi-modal fusion: vision AND touch, or vision alone?
  • Material awareness: can it tell cotton from denim from silk by touch?

THINK — "What if I do X?"

Reasons about consequences, plans action sequences, decides what to do before committing to movement. Takes input from SENSE through a reasoning and prediction pipeline.

  • Multi-step planning: can it plan five or more steps ahead on an unseen task?
  • Out-of-distribution handling: what happens when the material misbehaves?

ACT — "How do I move?"

Converts the plan into precise motor commands in real time. Generates smooth executable trajectories. Adapts in real time to contact forces.

  • Cross-embodiment transfer: does the policy work across different robot hardware without retraining?
  • Sim-to-real gap: measured performance loss from simulation to real world for deformables?

How the industry is building the THINK layer: three model families

Three model families dominate the landscape. They are not competing alternatives but complementary building blocks. The most capable systems stack all three. Today, most companies have built one or two. The world model layer remains the least mature and the hardest to build.

SENSE (perception hardware)

Vision and force sensing are commoditizing. Tactile is emerging.

  • Proprietary data generation: does the sensor create unique training data per deployment?
  • Multi-modal integration at the hardware level
  • Material discrimination by touch, not just vision

THINK (brain hardware)

Two major players: NVIDIA Jetson Thor (production standard), private custom silicon (Tesla AI5).

  • On-device inference: full THINK stack without cloud dependency?

ACT (motion + manipulation)

DOF arms race is real, but DOF without touch is a constraint, not a capability.

  • Drive architecture fit: compliance and backdrivability for fabric
  • Per-finger tactile sensing
  • Task-specific end-effector design vs general-purpose hand

DATA + SIMULATION: training methods for the brain

DATA

Physical interaction data cannot be downloaded from the internet. Must be collected episode by episode in real environments. Current robot datasets total roughly 5,000 hours combined. GPT-4 trained on 45TB of text. The shortfall exceeds 99%.

  • Provenance: open-source (generic) vs proprietary (real production)
  • Fabric specificity: percentage of training data that involves deformables
  • Sensor coverage: tactile + force, or vision only?
  • Collection infrastructure: repeatable pipeline at scale?

SIMULATION

Lets robots train at extraordinary speed without wearing out hardware. NVIDIA Cosmos can generate full manipulation trajectories from a single image and a language instruction.

  • Sim-to-real gap: measured error rate for fabric?
  • Deformable physics fidelity: drape, friction, elasticity per material type?
  • Sim-real feedback loop: real-world data calibrating the simulator?
Neither data nor simulation alone solves the training problem for fabric. The companies that will win combine both: real-world data to ground the system, simulation to scale it, and a feedback loop between the two. This is precisely where factory access becomes a strategic asset, not just a deployment site.

Section 3 · The Textile Challenge

Technical barriers stand between robots and garment manufacturing.

Garment automation is not a single problem. Robots face distinct technical barriers, each requiring different solutions.

Deformable object manipulation

Infinite configuration: garment shape changes constantly during handling. Infinite combination: the same push produces different results depending on configuration, material, temperature, humidity.

Precision

Sub-millimeter accuracy required. Seam allowances are 5–10mm. Thread paths are nearly invisible; needle placement at speed is beyond most industrial robots.

Occlusion

Wrinkles and folds block camera views at the moment decisions must be made. When a robot grips fabric, the contact point hides from visual sensors. Self-occlusion problem.

Variety

A single factory may handle dozens of fabric types (cotton, silk, polyester, blends). Garment styles change seasonally. A robot trained on one item often fails on the next.

Adaptability / skill

Context-dependent judgment: how much force to iron with, when to adjust sewing speed for curves, how to handle a collar differently from a cuff. Skills humans build over months.

Section 6 · Our View & Recommendation

We invest to build together, not just to hold a position.

Capital is the entry ticket. The value-add is everything else. The companies best positioned to win in textile EI are those that build in deep strategic alignment with a domain partner who understands what the problem actually requires.

Our engagement angle

  • Collaboration first. Investment can follow.
  • We value partnership more than market hype
  • Target: early-stage companies in the enabling layers (sensing, perception, domain-specific manipulation tools). Companies where our involvement changes what they can build, not just where they deploy.
  • The right partner values domain expertise as a strategic asset: our factories as data infrastructure, our SOPs as training material, our operational knowledge as a moat.

Humanoid? Our view

  • Humanoids are still expensive and best destined for applications with social/emotional value (domestic, retail, service, edutainment)
  • Humanoids are overkill for industrial automation, especially low-margin settings like garment
  • Our focus: modular approaches and purpose-built designs for form-task fit

What we bring to every partnership · what the right partner looks like

What we bring

  • Domain expertise. 40+ years of textile manufacturing knowledge that cannot be bought, simulated, or scraped.
  • Factory validation. Real production lines in China and Vietnam. The only environment where any EI claim is actually tested.
  • Data generation. Every robot deployment on our lines produces proprietary fabric-interaction data. Our factories are data infrastructure.
  • Commercialization path. qonvolv's track record taking factory-floor innovations to global markets. A portfolio company does not need to build a sales engine from scratch.

What the right partner looks like

  • Building in the enabling layers, not trying to own the full stack
  • Early enough that our contribution changes the trajectory of what they build
  • Willing to codevelop: shared IP frameworks, joint dataset contributions, genuine skin in the game
  • Understands textile automation as a complex domain problem to solve together, not a market to sell into

Invest, partner, or monitor: how we engage at each stage

Every company in our pipeline is assessed against one question: how much does our involvement change what they can build? The answer determines the engagement tier.

Monitor. Promising technology. Fit or stage not yet right for deeper engagement. Stay close; do not commit capital.
Partner. Critical capability at a specific layer. Valuable collaboration, but equity alignment is premature or too expensive. Structure as task collaboration, data-sharing, or MOU.
Invest. Deep strategic alignment. Mutual dependency. We change what they build; they change what we can do. Co-develop IP, share data, grow together.

What deep strategic alignment requires: building in enabling layers where our domain contribution is the unlock; valuation reflecting co-development upside not just financial return; IP and data terms creating joint ownership; a clear path for validated solutions to flow through qonvolv to external markets.
